How to choose a renovation company in Miami: 10 questions to ask before you sign

Choose the renovation company that answers ten plain questions in writing before you sign: who pulls the permit, what one fixed price includes, how payments follow the work, and how changes, lien releases, the condo board and the warranty are handled. A good answer is specific and on paper; a red flag is a vague promise, a price given over the phone or a large payment before anything is designed. Below is each question with the answer to expect and the warning sign, in one table you can take to every meeting. Who should pull the permit for my renovation?

The contractor who holds the Florida license should pull the permit for any work that moves plumbing, changes electrical circuits, opens or moves walls, replaces windows or changes air conditioning. Purely cosmetic work, such as paint and surface finishes, generally does not need one; if you are unsure, ask your local building department.

Be careful with two answers: “no permit needed” for work that touches plumbing, wiring or walls, and a request that you pull the permit yourself as an owner-builder, which in Florida puts the legal responsibility for the work on you, the owner. Check the name on the license on the state's online license lookup before you sign.

Should a renovation price be fixed and in writing?

Yes. A serious company gives you one itemized, fixed estimate in writing after it has seen the space, and the number changes only through a change order you sign first. A total quoted over the phone is a guess, because the building, the layout, the finish level and what is behind the walls all move the number.

Read the estimate line by line. An allowance is a placeholder sum for something not yet chosen, such as tile or fixtures. A quote built mostly on allowances only looks cheaper, and it is the most common reason two quotes for the same home look so different.

How should payments, lien releases and change orders work?

Payments should follow the work in milestones, with no large upfront payment; every change should be a written change order you sign before the work; and you should receive a lien release from every subcontractor as they are paid. These rules keep your money tied to work you can see.

In Florida, an unpaid subcontractor or supplier can file a lien against your property even if you paid the contractor in full. A lien release is their signed statement that they have been paid, so ask for one at each milestone.

A good change order names the work, the price and the effect on the schedule, and nothing is done until you sign it. If a company says extras will be settled at the end, you are agreeing to a number you have not seen. On M5 projects all three rules are standard.

Does the team know how Miami condo boards work?

If you live in a condo, ask this before anything else, because the building shapes the project before the design does. A team that knows condo work asks for your building's renovation rules and application form first, and plans around board approval, the COI, elevator bookings and noise hours.

The red flag is a start date offered before anyone has read your building's rules. Many boards approve plans only at scheduled meetings, deposits and insurance requirements vary, and work hours are set by the building, not by the crew. Ask what the company does when a board asks for changes, and listen for a process, not reassurance.

Who will be on site, and what happens after handover?

Before you sign, you should know the one person who answers for the project, how often you will see progress and what the written warranty covers. Subcontracting trades is normal; what matters is that one team owns the schedule, checks the work at every stage and walks the finished space with you.

At handover, expect final cleaning, a walkthrough and a punch-list of small fixes, followed by a written warranty. M5 projects come with weekly photo updates through handover, on-site checks at every stage and a written 1-year warranty.

How do I compare two renovation quotes fairly?

Compare quotes only after both companies have seen the space and answered the same ten questions in writing; until then you are comparing different scopes, not different prices. Then read the estimates line by line, mark every allowance and ask what is not included.

One honest caveat: no checklist removes every risk. In an older condo or house, wiring, plumbing and moisture behind the walls can change the plan once the walls are opened. A good company tells you that before you sign and handles it through a change order, instead of promising there will be no surprises.
